# Everything You Should Know About Orthotics and Prosthetics

## Introduction

Life can change fast after limb loss or a serious injury. Even a short walk can feel like a big task. The good news is that modern orthotics and prosthetics can bring back movement, work, and independence.

This guide comes from the clinical team at Genfinity O&P. It explains what these devices are, who needs them, and how fitting works. It also covers what they cost in the United States. You will find simple recovery tips too. It is written for patients, caregivers, and care teams.

## Key Takeaways

- Orthotics support body parts you still have. Prosthetics replace a missing limb.
- The Amputee Coalition estimates that more than 2.3 million Americans live with limb loss.

- Daily skin care and steady therapy make recovery smoother.

## What Are Orthotics and Prosthetics?

These are two linked fields of rehabilitative medicine. Both use custom devices to help people move better after an injury, an illness, or a condition present from birth.

### What Is Orthotics?

An orthosis is a device worn on the outside of the body. It supports, corrects, or protects a part you still have. Common examples include a back brace, a knee brace, and a custom foot insert. These devices do not replace anything. They guide the joint or muscle so it works better.

### What Is Prosthetics?

A prosthesis replaces a missing body part. It can be a leg, a foot, an arm, or a hand. The goal is to restore function and make daily tasks easier. A well-fitted device can also help you feel like yourself again.

### Orthotics and Prosthetics at a Glance

| Feature | Orthotics | Prosthetics |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Support, protect, or correct existing body parts | Replace missing body parts |
| Common devices | Braces, spinal supports, foot orthoses | Prosthetic legs, arms, hands, and feet |
| Main benefit | Improve stability, alignment, and movement | Restore function and independence |
| Who may need them | People with joint, muscle, nerve, or spinal conditions | People with limb loss or congenital limb differences |

## Who Needs Orthotics and Prosthetics in the US?

Many health needs lead people to these devices. Seeing the range shows how common this care is.

### Common Reasons for Prosthetic Devices

- Injuries from accidents or combat
- Vascular disease, including diabetes complications
- Cancer that needs limb removal
- Limb differences present at birth

### Common Reasons for Orthotic Devices

- Scoliosis and other spine conditions
- Cerebral palsy and nerve conditions
- Sports injuries and ligament damage
- Plantar fasciitis and flat feet
- Joint instability and recovery after surgery

### How Many Americans Are Affected?

In 2024, the Amputee Coalition released a major national study. It estimates that more than 2.3 million Americans live with limb loss. Another 3.4 million live with a limb difference. That means a limb that differs in size, shape, or structure from birth. Together, that is over 5.6 million people.

The study used insurance claims data. It could not count people who are uninsured or who get care through the VA or TRICARE. So the real number is likely higher. Experts expect demand to keep rising as the population ages and diabetes-related problems grow.

## Types of Orthotic Devices

Orthotic devices cover many parts of the body. Their names often use short codes that show which joints they support.

### Lower Limb Orthotics

Foot orthoses (FO), ankle-foot orthoses (AFO), and knee-ankle-foot orthoses (KAFO) are among the most common.

An AFO often helps people after a stroke, with cerebral palsy, or with foot drop. A well-fitted AFO can help you walk with more confidence and lower your fall risk.

### Spinal Orthotics

Spinal braces treat problems from scoliosis to recovery after surgery.

- The Boston brace is widely used for children with scoliosis.
- The older Milwaukee brace is used less often today.
- Adults may use a lumbar sacral orthosis (LSO) for long-term low back pain or after back surgery.

### Upper Limb Orthotics

Wrist-hand orthoses (WHO) and elbow braces help after nerve injuries or tendon repairs. They also help with carpal tunnel syndrome.

These devices keep joints in line and protect healing tissue. They still allow safe, controlled movement.

## Types of Prosthetics and How They Work

Prosthetics are grouped by the level of amputation and the function they restore. Better materials and microprocessors have raised the bar for modern limbs.

### Lower Limb Prosthetics

A transtibial prosthesis sits below the knee. A transfemoral prosthesis sits above it. People with a below-knee amputation often adapt faster because the knee joint is still there.

Some modern knees, such as the Ottobock C-Leg, use sensors to change resistance as you walk. They react to speed and terrain in real time. This can feel steadier on stairs and slopes.

### Upper Limb Prosthetics

Upper limb devices range from simple cosmetic hands to advanced myoelectric arms.

A myoelectric device reads small electrical signals from the muscles in your residual limb. Those signals move the hand or wrist.

Some hands, such as the i-Limb from Össur, move each finger and let users pick grip patterns in a phone app.

### Body-Powered vs. Myoelectric Prosthetics

#### Body-Powered Prosthetics

Cables and a harness link your body movement to the device. These systems are tough, light, and simple to repair.

#### Myoelectric Prosthetics

Electrodes read your muscle signals. They offer finer control. They also cost more and need battery charging.

#### Hybrid Prosthetics

A hybrid blends both ideas in one system. For example, it may pair a body-powered elbow with a myoelectric hand.

## How the Fitting Process Works for Orthotics and Prosthetics

Getting a device is not a single visit. It is a clear process that can take several weeks to a few months. The timeline depends on the device and your health.

### Step 1: Initial Assessment

A certified orthotist or prosthetist starts with a full review. They look at your medical history, strength, and range of motion. They measure your limb and ask about your goals.

A runner who wants to race again needs a different device than someone who wants to walk comfortably at home.

You can schedule a consultation with Genfinity O&P for a personal clinical evaluation.

### Step 2: Casting and Fabrication

Next, the clinician takes a cast or a digital scan to build an exact mold. Many clinics now use 3D scanning. It improves accuracy and can shorten build time.

The device is then made from materials such as carbon fiber, thermoplastic, or silicone.

### Step 3: Fitting and Adjustments

Once it is built, the device is tried on and tested. The clinician checks comfort, alignment, and function.

Prosthetic legs often involve gait analysis. Orthoses may involve movement tests. Expect more than one fitting before the fit is right.

### Step 4: Rehabilitation and Training

Wearing the device is only the start. You work with physical and occupational therapists on balance, strength, fall prevention, and daily skills.

Research links structured rehab with better function, so please do not skip it.

From a clinical view, patients who keep every early follow-up visit tend to catch fit problems sooner. A small pressure spot is easy to fix in week two. It is much harder to fix in month three.

## Materials Used in Modern Devices

Material choice shapes weight, comfort, and lifespan. Today, many devices use aerospace-grade materials and biomedical engineering.

### Common Materials and What They Do

| Material | Main Benefit | Common Use |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon fiber | Light, strong, returns energy | Prosthetic feet and AFOs |
| Thermoplastics | Heat-moldable for a custom fit | Braces and sockets |
| Titanium alloys | Strong and light | Joints and connectors |
| Silicone | Soft and skin-like | Liners and covers |
| 3D-printed polymers | Fast, lower-cost custom parts | Foot orthoses and some sockets |

## Expert Insight on Where the Field Is Going

Clinicians and engineers point to three areas of progress. They are sensor integration, nerve interfaces, and 3D printing.

### Neural-Linked Prosthetics

Dr. Hugh Herr of the MIT Media Lab leads research on bionic limbs. His work shows that linking a prosthesis to the body's nerves and muscles can give wearers a better sense of limb position.

Scientists call this sense proprioception. It moves the field from simple movement toward real feedback.

## Prosthetic Limb Cost, Insurance, and Access in the US

Cost is a real barrier for many families. Knowing the usual ranges helps you plan and ask better questions.

### What Devices Cost in the United States

Prices vary by clinic, state, and components. Always ask for a written quote. The ranges below reflect common US figures checked in October 2026.

### Typical US Price Ranges Before Insurance

| Device | Typical Price Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Custom orthotic or brace | $500 to $5,000 | Depends on complexity and body area |
| Basic below-knee prosthetic leg | $5,000 to $15,000 | Includes socket and foot |
| Above-knee mechanical leg | $10,000 to $35,000 and up | Knee unit adds to the price |
| Leg with microprocessor knee | $30,000 to $70,000 or more | Sensors adapt to walking speed |
| Myoelectric arm | About $40,000 and up | Advanced bionic limbs can pass $100,000 |

### Four Things That Drive Most of the Price

1. **Level of amputation:** For example, below the knee or above it.
2. **Components:** A microprocessor knee can add $20,000 or more.
3. **Materials:** Costs vary from plastics to carbon fiber and titanium.
4. **Custom work:** The amount of specialized fitting and fabrication required.

### What Medicare Covers

Medicare Part B covers medically necessary braces and artificial limbs when a Medicare-enrolled doctor orders them.

You first meet the yearly Part B deductible, which is $257 in 2026. After that, Medicare pays 80 percent of the approved amount. You pay the other 20 percent unless you have Medigap or other backup coverage.

Medicare also rates your activity level from K0 to K4. That rating helps decide which components it will approve.

Payment amounts are public in the CMS DMEPOS fee schedule. Medicare often allows a replacement device about every five years. An earlier change needs proof of medical need.

### Private Insurance, Medicaid, and the VA

Private plans vary a lot. Some cover only basic devices. Others cover advanced ones when you show medical need.

Several states have laws that require private plans to cover prosthetics, but the rules differ by state. Medicaid rules also differ by state.

Veterans can often get devices through the VA, which covers a wide range of advanced prosthetics.

### Tips to Avoid Insurance Denials

- Ask your doctor for clear notes on why you need the device and how it helps daily tasks.
- Use a supplier that is enrolled with your plan.
- Get prior authorization in writing when your plan requires it.
- Keep copies of every prescription and clinic note.
- If you are denied, file an appeal. A first denial is not always final.

## Practical Recovery Tips After You Get Your Device

Recovery does not end when you leave the clinic. Daily habits shape how well you do. These tips reflect common clinical advice. Always follow your own care team's plan.

### Protect Your Skin and Your Fit

#### Check Your Skin Every Day

Look at the skin where the device touches you. Use a mirror for spots you cannot see.

Redness that does not fade, blisters, or sores need a call to your clinician.

#### Keep Liners and Socks Clean

Wash liners as directed and let them dry fully. Replace worn socks. Clean, dry layers lower the risk of rashes and sores.

#### Watch for Size Changes

Your limb can change size, especially in the first months. A loose or tight fit is a sign to book an adjustment.

Never force a device that does not fit.

### Build Strength and Balance

Do the exercises your therapist gives you. Short, regular sessions beat rare long ones.

Core and hip strength help most people with leg devices. Upper body strength matters most for arm devices.

### Increase Wear Time Slowly

Start with short wear periods and add time as your skin and muscles adapt. Your clinician will give you a schedule.

Rushing is a common cause of skin trouble.

### Look After Your Mind

Mood changes are common after limb loss or injury. Many people also feel phantom sensations.

Talk about both with your care team. Peer support helps too, and the Amputee Coalition offers peer programs.

### A Simple Daily Routine

- Check your skin in the morning and at night.
- Clean your liner and let it dry.
- Wear your device on the schedule your clinician gave you.
- Do your therapy exercises.
- Note any pain or fit change and tell your clinician early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Long Does It Take to Get a Prosthetic Leg?

Most people wait several weeks to a few months from the first visit to a final fit. Therapy continues after that.

### Does Medicare Pay for Prosthetics and Orthotics?

Yes, when they are medically necessary and ordered by a Medicare-enrolled doctor. After the Part B deductible, Medicare pays 80 percent of the approved amount.

### What Is the Difference Between Orthotics and Prosthetics?

Orthotics support a body part you still have. Prosthetics replace a part that is missing.

### How Often Will I Need a New Device?

It depends on use, body changes, and wear. Many insurers allow a replacement about every five years. You may need adjustments much sooner.
